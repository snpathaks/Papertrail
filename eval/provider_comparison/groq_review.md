## Introduction
Transformer architectures, originally devised for natural language processing, have recently been adapted to multivariate time‑series forecasting. Their self‑attention mechanism captures long‑range temporal dependencies, yet the quadratic complexity of standard attention has motivated specialized designs. This review surveys five representative contributions that illustrate the current trajectory of transformer‑based forecasting: Informer [P1] introduces a timestamp‑only decoder to mitigate error accumulation; Autoformer [P2] replaces dot‑product attention with an auto‑correlation module for series‑level aggregation; FEDformer [P3] uses neural architecture search to discover memory‑efficient variants; PatchTST [P5] reduces token count via patching and channel‑independence; and a recent empirical study [P4] compares full transformers with linear replacements to assess the necessity of attention layers. Together, these works delineate trade‑offs among model expressiveness, computational efficiency, and forecasting accuracy in long‑term prediction.

## Thematic Analysis
### Lightweight Temporal Attention
These studies investigate the suitability of Transformer architectures for long‑term time‑series forecasting. P2 introduces Autoformer, which replaces self‑attention with an auto‑correlation mechanism to capture series‑level dependencies, reporting MSE 0.065 and MAE 0.189 on the ETT benchmark [P2]. P3 proposes FEDformer, employing neural architecture search to discover memory‑efficient Transformers for ETTm2, achieving MSE 0.557 and 0.239 on the same dataset, yet noting rapid performance degradation in extended horizons [P3]. P4 evaluates whether Transformers truly preserve temporal order by simplifying them to linear layers, finding that standard Transformers fail to model scale, bias, and trend on Electricity, ETTh2, and Exchange‑Rate data [P4].

### Token-Efficient Forecasting
The two studies, Informer (P1) and PatchTST (P5), both investigate transformer‑based architectures for long‑sequence time‑series forecasting. Each proposes a strategy to mitigate the computational burden of standard self‑attention: Informer introduces a decoder that predicts solely from the time stamp, thereby reducing error accumulation and avoiding full‑attention masking, yet still suffers from O(L²) memory usage [P1]; PatchTST applies patching to shrink the input token set and adopts channel‑independent processing to enhance efficiency, though its limitations are not reported [P5]. Both works benchmark on the ETTh1 dataset (P1) and, in the case of PatchTST, on a broader suite of datasets including Weather, Traffic, Electricity, ILI, ETTh2, ETTm1, and ETTm2 [P5]. Thus, the papers share a focus on transformer‑based long‑term forecasting, efficiency‑enhancing modifications, and evaluation on the ETTh1 benchmark.

## Comparison of Methodologies
Token‑efficiency and lightweight attention design represent two distinct strategies for scaling Transformer‑based time‑series models.  Token‑efficient methods, such as PatchTST, reduce the input length through patching and exploit channel‑independence to cut computation, thereby trading model size for fewer tokens [P5].  In contrast, lightweight attention approaches refine the attention mechanism itself: Autoformer introduces an auto‑correlation module that aggregates information at the series level [P2]; FEDformer employs Neural Architecture Search to discover memory‑ and compute‑efficient Transformer variants [P3]; and the study on Transformers’ effectiveness replaces attention layers with linear layers or even an “Embed + Linear” baseline to isolate temporal order effects [P4].  Performance figures cannot be directly compared because each work evaluates on different benchmarks—P1 reports MSE/MAE on ETTh1 only, P2 on ETT, P3 on ETTm2, and P4 lists a broad set of datasets but omits numerical results [P1][P2][P3][P4].  Architecturally, P2’s auto‑correlation discovers dependencies at the series level, P3’s NAS yields compact Transformers, P4 isolates temporal order, while P1’s decoder predicts solely from timestamps to avoid error accumulation, and P5’s patching strategy enhances efficiency [P1][P2][P3][P4][P5].

## Research Gaps
**Research Gaps**

The current literature exhibits several notable gaps. First, dataset coverage is limited; high‑confidence evidence shows that only three of the five examined studies employ the *etth1* benchmark, with few other datasets represented [P1][P4][P5]. This raises the question of how model performance generalizes across diverse time‑series domains. Second, medium‑confidence findings indicate that Transformer‑based approaches suffer rapid performance degradation over long horizons, failing to capture scale, bias, and trend dynamics in Electricity, ETTh2, and Exchange‑Rate data [P3][P4]. Third, the quadratic complexity of canonical self‑attention remains a bottleneck; medium‑confidence evidence suggests that existing efficiency‑driven designs still rely on short, fixed‑length inputs, limiting practical applicability [P1][P3]. Finally, low‑confidence observations reveal an absence of discussion on robustness, fairness, interpretability, and generalization, with only a single study addressing fairness [P4]. These gaps underscore the need for systematic investigations into dataset diversity, long‑horizon stability, linear‑complexity attention mechanisms, and broader evaluation criteria.

## Conclusion
Across the surveyed literature, only three of five studies employ the ETTh1 benchmark, with limited exploration of alternative datasets [P1][P4][P5]. Transformer‑based models consistently underperform on long‑horizon tasks, failing to capture scale and bias in Electricity and ETTh2 and struggling with aperiodic series such as Exchange‑Rate [P3][P4]. Moreover, the canonical self‑attention mechanism incurs quadratic time and memory costs, prompting many works to restrict input length [P1][P3]. Finally, robustness, fairness, interpretability, and generalization remain largely unaddressed, underscoring critical avenues for future research.
